# Tone

The voice of a narrated explainer that argues a case. Distilled from an audit of finished episodes against
conversational podcasts on the same material: the facts were right, the tone was wrong, and so was **where the honesty
sat**. The podcasts persuaded through wonder; the episodes read like a credibility audit. This document is the fix.
It applies to any subject where you advocate a position and have to stay trustworthy.

## The core rule: concentrate honesty, don't sprinkle it

A persuasive explainer earns trust by being **more honest than its critics expect**, not by overclaiming. But a hedge
after every strong result reads as uncertainty, and an evidence grade on every plate turns the episode into an
audit. The same honesty, gathered in one place, reads as a document with nothing to hide.

- **One honesty chapter per episode** ("What we don't know"). Every uncertainty, limitation and open question lives
  there, stated plainly. Nowhere else: no "to be fair", no "no promises for you", no "low certainty" right after a win.
- **Move, never drop.** When you take a caveat out of a line, move it to the honesty chapter. Keep a list of what moved
  where. A caveat that disappears is a credibility debt.
- **This binds the orchestrator too.** Its own edits, briefs and fact-check merges are where the rule broke in
  practice. A fact-check replaces a wrong or vague number in place; it never adds a qualifier to a winning line. Any
  new limitation it surfaces goes to the honesty chapter's list, with its source. Before sending a brief or a script
  edit, search your own text for qualifiers outside the honesty chapter.
- **In-voice qualifiers that are part of the fact stay**, said once and plainly: "in mice", "in ten people", "in
  cells". They are facts, not grades.
- **Absence of evidence is not evidence of harm.** Say "under-studied" once, in the honesty chapter. Never list
  hypothetical harms.
- **Overcaution costs your best arguments.** A fear-framed chapter has no room for its strongest result. If the best
  outcome in the literature is remarkable, say it with the protocol that produced it.

## Structure that persuades

- **Open on a reversal.** Name the common-sense picture (the body as a machine, the textbook rule), flip it, and
  promise the hidden mechanism. Promise a discovery, not an audit. No disclaimer first.
- **Wonder moments.** Say the mechanism as a marvel, then show it is literal ("it sounds like magic; it is chemistry").
  Voice a paradox, then resolve it within a few lines.
- **One household analogy per mechanism, kept all the way through** (a freezer for long-term stores, a guard at a
  door, a recycling plant). Don't switch metaphors mid-mechanism.
- **The skeptic's doubt as a beat, not a disclaimer.** In a single-narrator format it becomes a rhetorical question
  the narrator answers: "Wait: more water than fat?"
- **Signposts and phases.** Numbered phases ("mobilise, conserve, recycle, rebuild") let the viewer hold the shape.
- **Turn a limit into the hook for the next idea** ("scarcity builds nothing; the refeed does").
- **Bookends.** The close returns to the opening image.
- **Uncertainty as intrigue, once:** "the most interesting hypothesis is…" in one line, not a hedge on every claim.

## Myths and stamps

- **Myth cards only for myths the critics hold.** Never voice one of your own side's overclaims just to stamp it: it
  puts the rejected wording on screen and makes the episode argue against itself.
- **Rejected claims never enter**, not even as a myth card. Keep a project list of claims checked and rejected (with
  the corrected version), and check every script against it.
- Where a page is shown, highlight the **most persuasive true sentence** on it, not the most hedged one; both are
  verbatim, so choose.

## Safety: protocol, not prohibition

Safety statements are non-negotiable and complete. They are also calm. Gather them in one chapter (usually the
honesty chapter or the one after it) as typeset **protocol cards**, one statement per card, with the narration
saying the same complete sentence.

Sort every warning into exactly one bucket before writing it; blurring them is what makes a script read as
fear-mongering:

| Bucket | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **contraindicated** | do not do this |
| **requires monitoring** | do this, with a protocol |
| **under-studied** | we do not know. **Not the same as dangerous.** |
| **implementation error** | the method is not the problem; the execution was |

- **Prefer protocol to prohibition** wherever the evidence allows: "do this with monitoring and a written plan" is
  safer *and* more persuasive than "don't do this".
- The one hard line (a genuinely contraindicated, potentially fatal combination) gets the only red on screen. It
  earns it by being the only one.
- Never soften a hard safety statement to improve flow, and never move it out of the narration into on-screen text
  only.

## On-screen devices that drift the tone

| Drifts toward audit / fear | Use instead |
|---|---|
| Evidence-tier tags on every plate (MEASURED IN HUMANS, IN ANIMALS, HYPOTHESIS, NO SUPPORT) | The citation lower-third (author · journal · year · population). Population is a fact, not a grade. |
| Stamps on your own side's claims | Delete, or turn into the positive plate |
| "BUT WATCH OUT", "TO BE FAIR", "LOW CERTAINTY" headers | Nothing; the caveat moves to the honesty chapter |
| Hatched "danger" zones, dashed "?" boxes for unmeasured harms | Removed; one plain ledger line in the honesty chapter |
| Shouting "NOT DATA" labels | One small, uniform, low-contrast "SCHEMATIC" |

## Verification battery (before re-voicing or rendering a script change)

Check by **reading**, not only by grep (a regex will miss honest prose that uses different words, and will flag
hedges it does not know):

1. Every number on screen and in the voice matches the fact-check file and the cited paper.
2. Every non-negotiable safety statement for the subject is present, complete, in its one place.
3. Every load-bearing concession for the subject is present, in its one place.
4. Every rejected claim appears only as a refutation, if at all.
5. Every caveat removed from a line was moved, not dropped (compare with the move list).
6. No new reference was added without verified metadata; no identifier (DOI, PMID, page) was invented.
7. A rewrite that keeps every citation can still delete an argument or a warning: audit for weakened caveats and
   overstated claims line by line.
